# SBU-363-Money-and-Capital-Markets---Final-Paper-and-Presentation
An analysis of the U.S. Treasury yield curve as a leading indicator of economic recessions. This project applies interest rate theories to explain why inversions signal shifts in market sentiment, risk appetite, and future monetary policy.

Data Sources
FRED (Federal Reserve Economic Data): 10Y-2Y and 10Y-3M Treasury spreads (1960–2026).

Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago: Empirical research on monetary policy easing signals.

The Brookings Institution: Expert analysis on term premiums and inflation risk.

Course Materials: Financial Markets and Institutions (Mishkin & Eakins) and lecture data on loanable funds.

Key Findings
Theoretical Alignment: Inversions are rational reflections of the Unbiased Expectations Theory, signaling that the market anticipates future rate cuts due to economic cooling.

Historical Accuracy: Analysis of the 2008 Great Recession confirms the curve's predictive power, showing an inversion nearly 18 months before the market collapse.

The "Un-Inversion" Trap: Data suggests the most critical recession signal is the "steepening" (return to positive slope), which typically marks the transition from prediction to active economic contraction.

Banking Impact: Inversions harm commercial lenders by compressing Net Interest Margins (NIM), leading to tighter credit standards and reduced economic activity.
